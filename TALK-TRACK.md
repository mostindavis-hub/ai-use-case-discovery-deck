**TALK TRACK**

**How to discover your next AI use case**

Facilitator guide for the interactive deck: 8 slides, about 50 minutes, mixed audience

**The outcome.** By the end of the session, everyone has named one first use case they can prove in 90 days, with an owner, a baseline metric and a review date.

## How to use this guide

**SAY** The script. It is written to be spoken, so change the words freely.

**DO** What to click, drag or flip on screen.

**ASK THE ROOM** The workshop question shown on the slide, plus a facilitation tip.

## Before you present

- Open the deck full screen with an internet connection. It loads its presentation and chart libraries online.

- Move with the arrow keys or the page scroller at the bottom of the screen. Press **S** to open speaker view, which holds a short note for each slide.

- Every interactive element resets when you leave a slide and come back, so you can rehearse and replay without any clean-up.

- Bring two real numbers for slide 5: a team size and the weekly hours spent on one task. Real numbers beat the defaults every time.

- All use cases, scores and savings in the deck are illustrative. Say so early.

- For a remote session, ask people to keep a shared document open for their long list and their slide 8 commitment.

# Slide 1: Title and promise

**Time:** 3 minutes **On screen:** How to discover your next AI use case, Spark an idea card, four path chips

**SAY**

> Most teams do not have an AI idea problem. They have a pile of ideas and no fair way to choose between them. Today is about fixing that.
>
> By the end of this session you will have named one use case you can prove in 90 days, with an owner, a baseline and a review date. We get there in four steps: find, score, test and scale. We will spend most of our time on find and score, because that is where teams stall.
>
> Here is what a good use case sounds like: one job, one outcome. Adapt one approved message into every channel format. Mine past results for your next test hypothesis. Nothing about models or tools. Just work.

**DO**

1.  Click **Spark another** two or three times. Point out the green tag for marketing and content, the teal tag for experimentation, and the lens label on each idea.

1.  Hover over the card to pause it. Left alone, it moves to a new idea every few seconds.

2.  Mention the four path chips along the bottom. Each one jumps to its step: Find to slide 2, Score to slide 4, and Test and Scale to slide 7.

**Warm-up (optional):** Ask which of these ideas people could picture in their own team’s week. A show of hands is enough.

**Transition:** *Let’s start where every good use case starts: with the work, not the technology.*

# Slide 2: Start with the work, not the technology

**Time:** 8 minutes **On screen:** Four flip cards, Lenses explored meter

**SAY**

> There are four lenses for finding use cases, and they work faster than a blank whiteboard. I will read the question on each card. Call out your ideas before I flip it, then we will compare with the examples.
>
> Do not filter yet. Rough ideas are welcome at this stage. We score them in a moment.

**DO**

1.  Allow about two minutes per lens. Read the question on the front of the card and collect ideas from the room first.

1.  Flip the card (click it, or press Enter or Space when it has focus) and read out the two examples from the table below.

2.  Capture every idea on a shared list, however rough. The full process aims for 15 to 25 candidates.

3.  Watch the **Lenses explored** meter at bottom left. It counts up as cards are flipped and says how many are left. At four it prompts you to take the list to step 2.

| **Friction**   | Where does work wait, bounce back or get escalated?  | Campaign briefs loop through four reviewers before a first draft exists. | Test ideas sit in a backlog because nobody has time to scope them.     |
| **Repetition** | What do you rebuild the same way every week?         | Reformatting one approved message for email, social, web and paid.       | Writing hypotheses, variation copy and results summaries from scratch. |

# Slide 3: Four steps from long list to live use case

**Time:** 4 minutes **On screen:** Step tracker with Find, Score, Test and Scale

**SAY**

> Each step has a time box, an output and a gate question. The gate question is the important part. If you cannot answer it, you stay in the step.
>
> That one rule stops teams from scaling something they have not proven. It also gives leaders a clear moment to say yes or no.

**DO**

1.  Click each step node, or use **Next step**, to move the glowing marker along the track. The card below changes with each step.

1.  Walk the room through the four gate questions in the table below.

2.  After step 4 the button reads **Back to Find**. Use it to make the point that discovery is a loop: the next use case comes from your matrix.

# Slide 4: Check your readiness before you pick

**Time:** 6 minutes **On screen:** Five sliders, live radar chart, three level cards

**SAY**

> Before you pick a use case, check that you can deliver one. There are five dimensions: data access, process clarity, team skills, governance and risk, and sponsorship. I will ask you for a number from zero to ten on each, and I want an honest one.
>
> The slide opens on Exploring, with a score of 30. Plenty of teams start here, and it is a perfectly good place to be. It simply tells you to begin with assistive, low-risk work on data you already hold.
>
> Look at the radar. The shortest spoke is the dimension most likely to hold you back. Either fix it, or choose a use case that does not depend on it.

**DO**

1.  Ask the room for a score on each dimension and drag the matching slider. The radar reshapes as you drag and the score updates out of 100.

1.  Explain the maths: the five sliders add up to a total out of 50, shown as a percentage.

2.  Click the level cards to jump to a preset: **Exploring** (0–39), **Building** (40–69, preset score 54) and **Scaling** (70–100, preset score 78). The matching card highlights as the score crosses a threshold.

**Presenter note:** The Most orgs. here badge on Exploring is a judgement call, not a benchmark. Do not quote it as a statistic.

# Slide 5: Estimate the payoff before you rank

**Time:** 7 minutes **On screen:** Six input sliders, six result tiles, doughnut and line charts

**SAY**

> Return on token is the simplest way I know to compare use cases. It is the value of the time you free, divided by what you spend on AI. If a task returns nine times what it costs, it is worth doing. If it returns one and a half, it is probably not worth the change effort, however clever it sounds.
>
> On screen: a team of 12, each spending four hours a week on one task. AI can take over 40% of that. At a loaded cost of \$55 an hour and \$40 per person per month on AI, that frees about 83 hours a month, worth \$4,572 against \$480 of spend. That is a return on token of 9.5 times, and about \$44,500 of net value over 12 months.
>
> Look at the line chart. Spend starts in month one, but value ramps with adoption. That is why the first couple of months look slow, and why adoption matters as much as the model.

**DO**

1.  Ask for a real team size and the weekly hours on one task, then drag the sliders. The six tiles count up and both charts redraw.

1.  Switch the currency between \$, £ and €. This changes the symbol only. It does not convert amounts.

2.  Drag **Months to full adoption** from 3 to 6. Twelve-month net value falls from \$44.5k to about \$37.7k with nothing else changed.

3.  Point to the doughnut: at the default numbers, 90% of the value is kept after AI spend.

**OPTIONAL: OPAL TIE-IN**

> Return on token is also how Opal approaches model choice. Opal sits above the large language models, selects the right one for each task and manages prompts and context, so a simple job does not carry a premium model price.

**Presenter note:** Treat the defaults as a worked example, not a benchmark. Replace them with the room’s own numbers wherever you can.

# Slide 6: Tackle the top right first

**Time:** 10 minutes **On screen:** Priority matrix with 11 use cases, team filter, bubble-size switch, Top 3 list

**SAY**

> This is the page that settles the argument. The vertical axis is impact: the value if it works. The horizontal axis is feasibility: how ready your data, skills and guardrails are.
>
> Top right is Start here. Top left is Big bets: worth doing, but plan the enablers first. Bottom right is Fill-ins: easy wins with a small reward. Bottom left is Park it: revisit later.
>
> Bubble size adds a third view. On Readiness, a bigger bubble means the data is more ready. Switch to Risk and the biggest bubbles are the ones to treat with care.
>
> The score is not the answer. It starts the conversation about why we placed an idea where we did.

**DO**

1.  Start with bubble 2, **Multi-channel content adaptation**, which is selected when the slide opens. Its priority score is impact times feasibility divided by ten: 7 × 8 ÷ 10 = 5.6.

1.  Read the **Top 3 right now** list. It shows names and scores only, and it matches bubbles 2, 8 and 1. Ties go to higher data readiness, then lower risk, which is why bubble 2 sits above bubble 8 on the same 5.6.

2.  Click bubble 8, **Results summaries and next steps**, and drag **Impact** up to 10. The bubble climbs the chart, its score rises to 7.0 and it takes first place. Ask the room whether that feels right. Move to another slide and back to reset.

3.  Switch **Bubble size** to **Risk**. Bubbles 10 and 11 become the largest. Use them to separate a Big bet (always-on autonomous optimisation) from an idea to park (unreviewed autonomous publishing).

4.  Filter to **Marketing**, then **Experimentation**. The other team’s bubbles fade and the Top 3 re-ranks. Marketing: bubbles 2, 1 and 3. Experimentation: bubbles 8, 6 and 7.

5.  For the room’s own ideas, use a whiteboard with the same two axes. The deck’s eleven examples are placeholders.

>

>

**DO**

2.  Labels: below 40 is Fragile, 40–69 is Getting there, 70–89 is Solid, and 90 or more is Resilient.

3.  Mention that the Test and Scale chips on slide 1 land on this slide.

# Slide 8: Commit to your first use case

**Time:** 6 minutes **On screen:** Name box, six-item checklist, progress ring, commitment sentence

**SAY**

> Type your first use case into the box, then tick only what is true today.
>
> You do not need a perfect use case. You need one in the top right, with an owner, a baseline and a date when you will decide.
>
> Four lenses, five readiness dimensions, one matrix and one 90-day plan. Pick one, prove it small, then scale it.

**DO**

1.  Invite one volunteer to name their use case. As you type, your wording appears highlighted in green inside the sentence on the right, which ends with: hold a go or no-go review on day 60.

1.  Tick each statement the room can honestly claim. The ring fills towards 6/6 and the message counts down the commitments to go.

2.  At 6/6 the screen celebrates. If the room does not get there, that is fine: every unticked item becomes the follow-up action list.

## The six checklist statements

- We ran all four lenses and built a long list.

- We scored every idea on impact, feasibility, readiness and risk.

- Our first use case sits in the Start here quadrant.

- One owner, one baseline metric and one review date are named.

- Data access and guardrails are agreed.

- A 30-60-90 day plan exists, with a go or no-go at day 60.

# Likely questions and answers

**What if our readiness score is low?**

That is normal, and it changes the starting point rather than the destination. Choose a use case in the Start here quadrant with strong data readiness, keep a human review step, and use the pilot to build the skills and guardrails you are missing (slide 4).

**How accurate are the calculator numbers?**

They are a worked example. Replace them with your team’s real hours and loaded cost, then let the pilot baseline replace the estimate. The decision rule stays the same: does the value of the time freed clearly beat the spend? (slide 5)

**Why not start with the biggest bet?**

Big bets usually depend on enablers such as data access and governance. Start with a Start here use case to build evidence, skills and trust, and plan the enablers for the big bet in parallel (slide 6).

**How many use cases should we run at once?**

One to begin with. A single pilot with an owner and a baseline teaches you more than five half-started ones, and the matrix already holds your next candidate (slides 3 and 7).

**Does AI take over the whole task?**

No. The calculator assumes AI takes a share of the task (40% by default) and people review the output. The review rules you agree in the first 30 days keep quality and brand standards in place (slides 5 and 7).
